## Overview

A quantum computer with 100 good qubits cannot simulate a molecule with 500 spin orbitals. Every
realistic quantum-chemistry calculation therefore begins with two reductions that are chemistry,
not computer science: choosing which orbitals go on the quantum computer (the **active space**),
and choosing how the state within that space is parameterized (the **ansatz**). Get either wrong
and no amount of circuit fidelity rescues the answer.

This chapter is about those choices and about the accuracy standard they are judged against.
Chapter 06/01 defined **chemical accuracy** as `1 kcal/mol ≈ 1.6` mHartree and noted the shot
overhead it implies. Here that number is broken into an honest error budget — basis set, active
space, ansatz, optimizer, sampling, hardware — and each term is sized. The recurring lesson is
that the quantum part of the pipeline is usually not the dominant error, which is both reassuring
and a warning: a beautifully converged VQE inside a badly chosen active space is a precise answer
to the wrong question.

## Active Spaces and the Frozen Core

### The construction

Partition the molecular orbitals into three sets:

- **Inactive (core)** orbitals, held doubly occupied in every determinant.
- **Active** orbitals, in which electrons are correlated to all orders.
- **Virtual (secondary)** orbitals, held empty.

A complete active space with `n` electrons in `m` spatial orbitals is written **CAS(`n`,`m`)** and
needs `2m` qubits under a one-spin-orbital-per-qubit encoding, minus whatever Chapter 10/02's
tapering removes. The inactive orbitals do not vanish: they contribute a constant and a
mean-field potential that is folded into the active-space one-electron integrals,

```
E_core = Σ_{i∈inactive} 2h_ii + Σ_{ij∈inactive} [2(ii|jj) - (ij|ji)]
h'_pq  = h_pq + Σ_{i∈inactive} [2(pq|ii) - (pi|iq)]
```

so the active-space Hamiltonian has the same form as Chapter 10/01's with `h → h'` and
`E_nuc → E_nuc + E_core`. **Freezing the core** — treating the chemically inert `1s` shells this
way — is the cheapest and least controversial active-space choice: the oxygen `1s` in H₂O carries
enormous energy but essentially no chemistry.

### Why this is the binding constraint

The classical cost of CAS is the determinant count `C(m, n/2)²` for a closed-shell singlet:

| active space | determinants | qubits (JW) |
|---|---|---|
| CAS(2,2) | 4 | 4 |
| CAS(8,8) | 4,900 | 16 |
| CAS(12,12) | 853,776 | 24 |
| CAS(16,16) | 1.66 × 10⁸ | 32 |
| CAS(20,20) | 3.41 × 10¹⁰ | 40 |
| CAS(54,54) | 3.79 × 10³⁰ | 108 |

Classical CASSCF is routine to about CAS(16,16) and heroic beyond CAS(20,20); DMRG extends the
reach for quasi-1D orbital couplings but not in general. A quantum computer's value proposition is
exactly the gap between the last two rows: `108` qubits versus `10³⁰` determinants.

### Choosing the orbitals

There is no algorithm that is right in all cases, but there are reliable diagnostics:

- **Natural orbital occupation numbers (NOONs)** from a cheap correlated method (MP2 or CCSD).
  Orbitals with occupations near `2` or `0` are uninteresting; those in the window `[0.02, 1.98]`
  are strongly correlated and belong in the active space.
- **Orbital entanglement entropy** from a small DMRG run — the single-orbital von Neumann entropy
  `s_p` and the mutual information `I_pq` identify the strongly coupled block directly.
- **Chemical intuition**: the `3d` shell of a transition metal, the `π` system of a conjugated
  chain, the bonds being broken along a reaction coordinate.
- **Consistency along a reaction path**: an active space that changes composition between reactant
  and transition state produces discontinuous energies and meaningless barriers.

### CASSCF in one paragraph

CASSCF (complete active space self-consistent field) solves two coupled problems at once: the FCI
problem inside the active space, and the optimization of the orbitals themselves. The orbital
rotation is generated by an antisymmetric matrix `κ` through `C → C exp(κ)`, with `M(M-1)/2`
independent parameters, and CASSCF alternates (or jointly converges) the CI coefficients and `κ`
until the energy is stationary with respect to both. The reason it matters here is that
*Hartree-Fock orbitals are the wrong orbitals for a small active space*: they are optimized for a
single determinant, and reoptimizing them in the presence of correlation often recovers more energy
than adding two more orbitals. CASSCF misses dynamic correlation from outside the active space,
which is why it is usually followed by a perturbative correction (CASPT2, NEVPT2).

## Ansätze

### UCCSD

The unitary coupled cluster ansatz of Chapter 06/01, `|ψ(θ)⟩ = exp(T(θ) - T†(θ))|HF⟩`, has one
amplitude per spin- and symmetry-allowed excitation. For a closed-shell system with `n_o` occupied
and `n_v` virtual spatial orbitals the counts are exactly

```
singles = 2 n_o n_v
doubles = 2 C(n_o,2) C(n_v,2) + (n_o n_v)²
```

| system | `M` | `e⁻` | qubits | singles | doubles | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ / STO-3G | 2 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 1 | **3** |
| LiH / STO-3G | 6 | 4 | 12 | 16 | 76 | 92 |
| H₂O / STO-3G (frozen core) | 6 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 76 | 92 |
| H₂O / STO-3G | 7 | 10 | 14 | 20 | 120 | 140 |
| N₂ / STO-3G | 10 | 14 | 20 | 42 | 567 | 609 |

H₂'s three parameters match the table in Chapter 06/01. The growth is `O(M⁴)`, and because each
amplitude becomes 2 (singles) or 8 (doubles) Pauli exponentials, each of which needs a CNOT ladder,
circuit depth grows as `O(M⁴)` to `O(M⁵)` — the reason UCCSD is unrunnable beyond toy molecules on
NISQ hardware. Two further wrinkles: `exp(T - T†)` must be Trotterized, so the circuit depends on
the operator ordering (a small but real ansatz-definition ambiguity), and unlike classical CCSD,
UCCSD has no cheap non-variational evaluation, which is precisely why a quantum computer is wanted.

### k-UpCCGSD

Lee, Huggins, Head-Gordon and Whaley's **k-UpCCGSD** keeps only *paired* doubles — both electrons
of a spatial orbital move together — but allows **generalized** excitations (any orbital to any
orbital, not just occupied-to-virtual) and repeats the whole block `k` times with independent
parameters. The parameter count collapses to `k·M(M-1)` — `O(kM²)` rather than `O(M⁴)`:

| system | UCCSD | k-UpCCGSD (`k = 3`) |
|---|---|---|
| LiH / STO-3G | 92 | 90 |
| H₂O / STO-3G | 140 | 126 |
| N₂ / STO-3G | 609 | 270 |

The trade is expressibility per parameter for depth: `k` is a convergence knob, and `k = 3` to `5`
typically reaches chemical accuracy on small molecules with far shallower circuits than UCCSD. The
restriction to paired doubles makes the ansatz exact for seniority-zero (perfect-pairing) physics
and systematically deficient for anything requiring open-shell character, which the generalized
singles only partly repair.

### Hardware-efficient ansätze, and why chemistry resists them

A hardware-efficient ansatz (Chapter 06/02) uses `L` layers of single-qubit rotations and the
device's native entangler: `2nL` parameters, minimal depth, no chemistry. Three problems follow.
It does not conserve particle number, `S_z` or `S²`, so the optimizer wanders into unphysical
sectors and the "ground state" it finds may have the wrong electron count. Its expressibility is
generic rather than targeted, so it hits barren plateaus (Chapter 06/05) as `n` and `L` grow. And
it has no systematic improvability: if `L` layers are not enough, `L+1` is a guess, not a
correction. Hardware-efficient circuits are excellent for hardware benchmarking and poor for
quantum chemistry — a distinction the early VQE literature sometimes blurred.

### Orbital-optimized VQE

The same insight that makes CASSCF better than CASCI applies on a quantum computer. In **oo-VQE**
the classical optimizer carries the orbital-rotation parameters `κ` alongside the circuit
parameters `θ`, minimizing `E(θ, κ)` jointly. Each `κ` update is a classical integral
transformation — no extra circuit depth at all — and it buys a great deal: orbital optimization
frequently recovers more energy than doubling the ansatz depth, and it can make a *shallower*
ansatz sufficient. It also removes an embarrassment: without it, a VQE result depends on which
classical program supplied the orbitals.

## What Chemical Accuracy Actually Demands

Chemical accuracy is `1 kcal/mol = 1.5936 mHartree = 43.5 meV` — the precision at which a computed
reaction barrier predicts a rate to within a factor of a few at room temperature. It is a target
for *relative* energies, which is easier than it sounds, because systematic errors partly cancel
between reactant and product. The full error budget:

| source | typical size | fixed by |
|---|---|---|
| basis-set incompleteness | 10–100 mHa absolute; 1–10 mHa relative | bigger basis, F12, extrapolation — classical |
| active-space truncation | 1–50 mHa relative | more orbitals, or a perturbative correction |
| ansatz (UCCSD vs FCI in-space) | 0.1–5 mHa | deeper ansatz, ADAPT, oo-VQE |
| optimizer convergence | user-chosen | tighter tolerance, better gradients (06/03) |
| shot noise | `λ/√S` per energy evaluation | more shots, Pauli grouping, shadows |
| hardware noise | 10–1000 mHa raw | error mitigation (06/06), then error correction |

Only the last three are the quantum computer's problem, and note the ordering: on today's
hardware, raw noise exceeds chemical accuracy by two to four orders of magnitude, which is why
every serious VQE demonstration leans on error mitigation.

The shot requirement follows from the Pauli decomposition. With `H = Σ_i c_i P_i` and independent
estimates of each `⟨P_i⟩`, the worst-case variance of one energy evaluation is bounded by
`(Σ_i |c_i|)²/S`, so

```
S ≈ (λ / ε)²,   λ = Σ_i |c_i| = ||H||₁
```

For the tapered H₂ Hamiltonian of Chapter 10/02, `λ = 0.988` Ha, so `ε = 1.6` mHa needs
`S ≈ 3.8 × 10⁵` shots *per energy evaluation*; for the four-qubit form, `λ = 1.894` Ha and
`S ≈ 1.4 × 10⁶`. Multiply by a few hundred optimizer iterations and a molecule with `λ ~ 100` Ha,
and the `10⁹`–`10¹²` shot counts that make VQE's scaling uncomfortable appear immediately. Grouping
commuting Paulis and classical shadows reduce the prefactor; they do not change the `1/ε²` law,
which is the deep reason Chapter 10/04's phase-estimation methods — with their `1/ε` Heisenberg
scaling — eventually win.

## Key Formulas

- **Active-space core folding**: `h'_pq = h_pq + Σ_{i∈core} [2(pq|ii) - (pi|iq)]`
- **CAS dimension**: `C(m, n_α) × C(m, n_β)`; qubits `= 2m` before tapering
- **UCCSD counts**: `2n_o n_v` singles, `2C(n_o,2)C(n_v,2) + (n_o n_v)²` doubles
- **k-UpCCGSD count**: `k·M(M-1) = O(kM²)`
- **Orbital rotation**: `C → C exp(κ)`, `κ = -κᵀ`, `M(M-1)/2` parameters
- **Chemical accuracy**: `1 kcal/mol = 1.5936 mHa = 43.5 meV`
- **VQE shot cost**: `S ≈ (λ/ε)²` with `λ = Σ_i|c_i|`

## Worked Example: UCCSD on H₂, and Where the Active Space Bites

**One amplitude.** For H₂/STO-3G the only symmetry-allowed amplitude is the double excitation
`(σ_g α, σ_g β) → (σ_u α, σ_u β)`: the singles connect `g` to `u` and are forbidden by inversion
parity. Building `G = i(T₂ - T₂†)` from the Jordan-Wigner images and exponentiating it:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.circuit import Parameter
from qiskit.circuit.library import PauliEvolutionGate
from qiskit.primitives import StatevectorEstimator
from qiskit.quantum_info import SparsePauliOp

N = 4
def ann(j, n=N):                       # Jordan-Wigner image of a_j
    return SparsePauliOp(["I"*(n-j-1) + "X" + "Z"*j, "I"*(n-j-1) + "Y" + "Z"*j],
                         coeffs=[0.5, 0.5j])
a = [ann(j) for j in range(N)]; ad = [o.adjoint() for o in a]

terms = {"IIII": -0.81054799, "ZIII": +0.17218394, "IZII": -0.22575348,
         "IIZI": +0.17218394, "IIIZ": -0.22575348, "ZZII": +0.12091263,
         "ZIZI": +0.16892754, "ZIIZ": +0.16614543, "IZZI": +0.16614543,
         "IZIZ": +0.17464342, "IIZZ": +0.12091263, "XXXX": +0.04523280,
         "XXYY": +0.04523280, "YYXX": +0.04523280, "YYYY": +0.04523280}
H = SparsePauliOp([k[::-1] for k in terms], coeffs=list(terms.values()))
E_nuc = 0.71996899

T2 = ad[1] @ ad[3] @ a[2] @ a[0]                   # (0a, 0b) -> (1a, 1b)
G = (1j * (T2 - T2.adjoint())).simplify(atol=1e-12)

theta = Parameter("t")
qc = QuantumCircuit(N)
qc.x(0); qc.x(2)                                   # |HF>: orbitals 0 and 2 occupied
qc.append(PauliEvolutionGate(G, time=theta), range(N))
qc = qc.decompose(reps=3)
print("generator terms:", len(G), " CNOTs:", qc.count_ops()["cx"])

est = StatevectorEstimator()
energy = lambda t: float(est.run([(qc, H, [t])]).result()[0].data.evs) + E_nuc
res = minimize_scalar(energy, bounds=(-np.pi, np.pi), method="bounded",
                      options={"xatol": 1e-12})
E_fci = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H.to_matrix()).real.min() + E_nuc
print(f"E_HF  = {energy(0.0):.8f} Ha")
print(f"E_VQE = {res.fun:.8f} Ha  at theta = {res.x:+.6f}")
print(f"E_FCI = {E_fci:.8f} Ha    error = {1e6*(res.fun - E_fci):.3f} microHa")
```

Output (Qiskit 2.5.2):

```
generator terms: 8  CNOTs: 48
E_HF  = -1.11699900 Ha
E_VQE = -1.13730604 Ha  at theta = -0.111769
E_FCI = -1.13730604 Ha    error = 0.000 microHa
```

One parameter, exact to machine precision. The optimum `θ* = -0.111769` is not an arbitrary number:
it is the CI mixing angle, so the state is `cos θ* |σ_g²⟩ - sin θ* |σ_u²⟩` with
`|c_uu|² = sin²θ* = 0.0124`. The 48 CNOTs for a *single* amplitude are the honest cost of a
Trotterized eight-Pauli generator, and they scale as `O(M⁴)` amplitudes each needing a ladder.

**Where a minimal active space breaks.** CAS(2,2) is exact within STO-3G for H₂ at every geometry,
so stretching the bond isolates the *physics* of active-space choice from basis-set error:

| `R` (Å) | `E_HF` (Ha) | `E_FCI` (Ha) | `E_corr` (mHa) | σ_u² weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.735 | -1.116999 | -1.137306 | -20.3 | 0.012 |
| 1.000 | -1.066109 | -1.101150 | -35.0 | 0.031 |
| 1.500 | -0.910874 | -0.998149 | -87.3 | 0.126 |
| 2.000 | -0.783793 | -0.948641 | -164.9 | 0.288 |
| 2.500 | -0.702944 | -0.936055 | -233.1 | 0.406 |
| 3.000 | -0.656048 | -0.933632 | -277.6 | 0.463 |

At equilibrium the correlation energy is `-20` mHa and the wavefunction is `98.8 %` one
determinant: a single-reference problem, where classical CCSD(T) is essentially exact and a quantum
computer adds nothing. By `3.0 Å` the correlation energy is `-278` mHa — 174 times chemical
accuracy — and `|c_uu|²` has climbed to `0.463`, approaching the `0.5` of two independent hydrogen
atoms. Hartree-Fock's error is now larger than the bond energy itself. This is **static (strong)
correlation**: no single determinant is a good reference, CCSD(T) fails qualitatively, and the
active space *must* contain both orbitals. Every serious quantum-advantage target in chemistry —
transition-metal clusters, bond-breaking transition states, polyradicals — is at the bottom of this
table, not the top.

## Summary

- Active-space selection is the first and largest approximation: inactive orbitals are folded into
  `h'_pq` and a constant `E_core`, and only the active block reaches the quantum computer.
- Classical CAS is feasible to roughly CAS(16,16) (`1.7 × 10⁸` determinants); CAS(54,54) needs
  `3.8 × 10³⁰` determinants but only 108 qubits — the whole quantum value proposition.
- CASSCF reoptimizes orbitals together with CI coefficients; the same trick as oo-VQE buys accuracy
  on a quantum computer at zero extra circuit depth.
- UCCSD has `O(M⁴)` amplitudes (3 for H₂, 609 for N₂/STO-3G); k-UpCCGSD reduces this to `O(kM²)` at
  the price of paired-only doubles; hardware-efficient ansätze break particle-number and spin
  symmetry and are not systematically improvable.
- Chemical accuracy (`1.5936` mHa) is a relative-energy target, and shot noise alone costs
  `S ≈ (λ/ε)²` — already `1.4 × 10⁶` shots per energy evaluation for four-qubit H₂.
- Weakly correlated molecules are solved classically; the quantum targets are the strongly
  correlated ones, where `|c_uu|²`-style multireference character is large.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1**: A transition-metal complex has 120 spatial orbitals and 96 electrons. You pick
CAS(14,14) with the metal `3d` and ligand `σ` orbitals. How many qubits does it need, how many
determinants would the classical CI have, and what error source have you certainly *not* removed?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Qubits: `2 × 14 = 28` before tapering, `26` after removing the two particle-number symmetries of
Chapter 10/02. Determinants: `C(14,7)² = 3432² = 11,778,624` — a small classical CASCI, well under
a second on a laptop.

What remains is **dynamic correlation**: the correlation energy of the 82 electrons in the 106
orbitals outside the active space. That is typically hundreds of mHa in absolute terms and easily
tens of mHa in relative terms — an order of magnitude above chemical accuracy. It is treated
classically by CASPT2/NEVPT2 on top of the active-space wavefunction, which is why a quantum
computer solving CAS(14,14) perfectly does not by itself produce a chemically accurate number.

</details>

**Exercise 2**: For H₂O/STO-3G with a frozen oxygen `1s` core, verify the UCCSD parameter counts in
the table and give the number of Pauli exponentials the circuit contains.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Frozen core leaves `M = 6` spatial orbitals and 8 electrons, so `n_o = 4`, `n_v = 2`:

```
singles = 2 n_o n_v = 2 × 4 × 2 = 16
doubles = 2 C(4,2) C(2,2) + (4 × 2)² = 2 × 6 × 1 + 64 = 12 + 64 = 76
total   = 92 amplitudes
```

matching the table. Each single contributes 2 Pauli strings and each double contributes 8, so one
Trotter step is `16 × 2 + 76 × 8 = 640` Pauli exponentials — before the CNOT ladders that implement
each one, and before any repetition for Trotter error. Compare the 48 CNOTs the worked example
needed for *one* amplitude.

</details>

**Exercise 3**: Your VQE gives `E = -76.0231` Ha for a molecule whose experimental value is
`-76.4376` Ha. Before blaming the quantum computer, list what you would check, in order.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The `414` mHa gap is 260 times chemical accuracy, far too large to be circuit noise on a
well-mitigated small system. Check, cheapest first:

1. **Nuclear repulsion and core energy.** A missing `E_nuc` or `E_core` is a clean constant offset
   and the commonest bug (Chapter 10/01).
2. **Basis set.** STO-3G for a 10-electron molecule is hundreds of mHa from the complete-basis
   limit. Experimental total energies are only comparable at (or extrapolated to) that limit.
3. **Active space.** Compare against the classical CASCI energy for the *same* active space. If
   VQE matches CASCI, the ansatz and hardware are fine and the active space is the error.
4. **Symmetry sector.** If tapering was used, confirm the sector from `⟨Φ_HF|τ_m|Φ_HF⟩`; the wrong
   sector gives a clean, converged, wrong answer.
5. **Ansatz and optimizer.** Only now: compare against a classical statevector simulation of the
   same circuit to separate ansatz error from hardware error.

The variational principle gives one free check throughout: any energy *below* the exact FCI energy
of the active space means a bug, not a discovery.

</details>

**Exercise 4**: The tapered two-qubit H₂ Hamiltonian has `λ = 0.988` Ha; the four-qubit
Jordan-Wigner form has `λ = 1.894` Ha. Compare the shots each needs for `ε = 1.6` mHa, and explain
why tapering helps twice.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`S ≈ (λ/ε)²` gives `(0.988/0.0016)² = 3.8 × 10⁵` shots versus `(1.894/0.0016)² = 1.4 × 10⁶` — a
factor of `3.7`, which is `(1.894/0.988)²`.

Tapering helps twice because removing a qubit both deletes Pauli terms (the `1`-norm shrinks, since
terms that differed only on the tapered qubit merge or cancel) and shortens the circuit that must
be run for each remaining term. The second effect is the one that matters on hardware: fewer
qubits means fewer entangling gates and less decoherence per shot, so each of the `3.8 × 10⁵` shots
is also more accurate. Note the limit of the argument — the `1/ε²` scaling is untouched, so
tapering changes the constant, never the asymptotics.

</details>
